# The Neo-Alexandria Manifesto

When we want to understand something, someone who knows the field can change what we’re able to learn. They may know where to find the evidence, how to make sense of conflicting accounts, or why a question remains open. Some of what they know may have been passed from person to person and never written down.

Neo-Alexandria is an open, decentralized protocol for sharing knowledge. Anyone can ask a human or AI expert to recommend a source that speaks to a question in their field. They can also offer a price for the expert to write something new, whether that means explaining a difficult idea, drawing together scattered findings, or recording what experienced practitioners know.

The protocol is token-free and carries no protocol fees. If you commission a piece of work, you and the expert agree on the price, how to pay, and how the result can be shared. When you both choose to make it public, something one person paid to understand becomes available to the next person with a similar question.

That is the promise we take from the Library of Alexandria. We want what people learn to be available for others to discover, question, and build on. This library can grow wherever people and agents exchange knowledge, across the systems and communities they choose for themselves.

We mean permissionless in the way email is permissionless. You can run a client yourself or choose a provider. Either way, you can reach people on independently operated systems without registering with a central authority. Recipients decide whose messages to accept and which requests to take on. People can ask the experts whose work they trust, and communities can decide for themselves how to recognize expertise.

The freedom to run your own client depends on a public specification that defines how peers communicate and what a secure client must do. Anyone should be able to give the specification to a coding agent and generate a complete client they can verify and run. That puts building a client within reach of more people, including those whose needs differ from those of the original developers.

We want many independently built clients in everyday use. They can share the rules of the protocol while using different code, libraries, and languages. When a flaw is specific to one implementation, people using others can keep exchanging work. That is what we mean by security through diversity.

If you have a question you’ve struggled to get answered or knowledge you’ve had trouble passing on, we invite you to help shape this library.
