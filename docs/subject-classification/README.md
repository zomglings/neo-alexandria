# Subject classification

A shared topic vocabulary lets two catalogs describe the same subject with the same identifier, even if their users search for it in different words. Libraries use subject classifications to arrange collections and subject headings to find works across them. The same kind of vocabulary can describe an area of expertise. Assigning a topic to a record and deciding which topics belong in the vocabulary are separate decisions.

## Library of Congress

**Library of Congress Classification (LCC).** A cataloger identifies a work’s predominant subject and consults the classification schedules for the most specific applicable number. The schedule may give instructions for works covering several subjects. When several numbers still fit, the cataloger considers the author’s intent and where the work would be most useful. For example, the science schedule lists `QR51` for agricultural microbiology and directs catalogers to the `QR111` range for soil microbiology. The full call number adds a book number, often based on the author’s surname, to distinguish works in the same class. [Classification principles](https://www.loc.gov/aba/publications/FreeCSM/F010.pdf) · [Science schedule](https://www.loc.gov/aba/publications/FreeLCC/LCC_Q2025TEXT.pdf#page=471) · [Call numbers](https://www.loc.gov/aba/publications/FreeCSM/G053.pdf)

Catalogers at other libraries can assign LCC numbers to their own records. Library of Congress catalogers and institutions participating in the Subject Authority Cooperative Program (SACO) can formally propose new or revised numbers. A proposal identifies where the number belongs in the schedule and can cite the work that prompted it. Anyone can comment on the tentative lists. The Library of Congress policy division reviews proposals and publishes approved changes. [Assignment by other agencies](https://www.loc.gov/marc/bibliographic/bd050.html) · [Classification proposal instructions](https://www.loc.gov/aba/publications/FreeCSM/F050.pdf) · [Public review](https://loc.gov/aba/pcc/saco/saco-shorts.html) · [Approved lists](https://www.loc.gov/aba/cataloging/classification/weeklylists/)

**Library of Congress Subject Headings (LCSH).** A cataloger assigns controlled phrases for the subjects a work covers, putting the heading or headings for the predominant topic first. A heading should be as specific as the work warrants. Where the rules allow it, catalogers can add subdivisions to express details such as place or period. The first headings often correspond to the class number because both describe the work’s main subject. Several headings can make one work findable through several subjects. [Assigning and constructing headings](https://www.loc.gov/aba/publications/FreeSHM/H0180.pdf) · [Order of headings](https://www.loc.gov/aba/publications/FreeSHM/H0080.pdf)

Library of Congress or SACO catalogers can submit formal proposals for new or revised headings. They research the term and cite sources. Anyone can suggest improvements or comment on pending proposals. The Library of Congress policy division reviews the proposals and adds approved changes to its subject authority file. [Subject heading proposal process](https://www.loc.gov/aba/cataloging/subject/lcsh-process.html)

## Dewey Decimal Classification

A cataloger determines the work’s subject, the discipline it serves, and, where relevant, its approach or form. The Relative Index suggests numbers under different disciplines for the same subject. The cataloger checks those suggestions against the schedules and their notes. Where the schedule provides instructions, the cataloger can build a more specific number by combining notation from schedules or tables. For example, the schedule for microorganisms directs a work on the microbiology of the human body to `612.001579`, under human physiology. [Introduction to Dewey](https://www.oclc.org/content/dam/oclc/dewey/versions/print/intro.pdf) · [Schedule for microorganisms](https://www.oclc.org/content/dam/oclc/dewey/updates/ddc23/579_20180125_DDC23.pdf)

Dewey users can propose changes and comment on drafts. OCLC’s Dewey editors develop revisions with classifiers at the Library of Congress and publish drafts as exhibits. The international Dewey Editorial Policy Committee reviews the proposals, and OCLC publishes changes in WebDewey. [Dewey Program](https://www.loc.gov/aba/dewey/about.html) · [Editorial Policy Committee](https://www.oclc.org/en/dewey/resources/epc.html) · [Example WebDewey update](https://community.oclc.org/t5/dewey-services/changes-from-epc-147-live/ba-p/66694)

## Internet Archive Open Library and Open Book Genome

**Open Library** groups editions under a work record. Each edition can carry its own publication details and classification numbers. Open Library can import a Library of Congress call number from MARC field `050` and extract topical subject strings from field `650`. That copies assignments from the source catalog. [About Open Library](https://openlibrary.org/about) · [Works and editions](https://openlibrary.org/dev/docs/api/books#learnings-about-works-v-editions) · [Classification import](https://github.com/internetarchive/openlibrary/blob/master/openlibrary/catalog/marc/parse.py) · [Subject import](https://github.com/internetarchive/openlibrary/blob/master/openlibrary/catalog/marc/get_subjects.py)

Anyone with an Open Library account can edit catalog records. Automated edits use a named bot account, and Open Library expects a human to review edits and records staged for import. Merging duplicates or deleting records requires additional permissions and training. Librarians-in-training can propose merges, and a Super-Librarian reviews and completes approved merges. [Editing guidelines](https://openlibrary.org/help/faq/editing) · [Librarians-in-training guide](https://openlibrary.org/librarians-in-training)

**Open Book Genome** develops tags for discovery across works, including genre, mood, and literary tropes. Contributors can propose a term in a public GitHub issue with a definition, example works, and a rationale. After discussion and rough consensus, a contributor opens a pull request to update the vocabulary files. Tightly controlled lists, such as literary form and content warnings, require explicit maintainer approval. Contributors can also propose mappings from existing Open Library subject strings to canonical tags, including mappings for Library of Congress headings found in catalog records. [Tag vocabulary](https://github.com/Open-Book-Genome-Project/tags) · [Contribution guide](https://github.com/Open-Book-Genome-Project/tags/blob/main/CONTRIBUTING.md)

## General Finnish Ontology (YSO)

The National Library of Finland maintains a multilingual, cross-disciplinary vocabulary for indexing. Anyone can propose a concept or a revision through a public form. Proposals become GitHub issues, and the vocabulary data is maintained in Git. Finto AI also uses YSO to suggest subjects for a piece of text. [YSO](https://finto.fi/yso/en/) · [Public proposals](https://github.com/Finto-ehdotus/YSE/issues) · [Vocabulary repository](https://github.com/NatLibFi/Finto-data) · [Finto AI](https://www.kansalliskirjasto.fi/en/services/finto-ai)

## Wikidata

Wikidata is a general-purpose knowledge graph. Items have identifiers and can have multilingual labels, aliases, and relationships. An item qualifies for inclusion if it has an eligible Wikimedia link, represents a clearly identifiable entity that public sources can describe, or serves a structural need in the graph. Editors classify items with statements such as `instance of` and `subclass of`. A class can have more than one parent. The structured data is available under CC0. [Item structure](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Help:Items) · [Notability policy](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Wikidata:Notability) · [Classification guidelines](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Help:Classification) · [Licensing](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Wikidata:Licensing)

Ordinary contributors can create eligible items and edit classification statements on most items. Logged-out editors receive temporary accounts. Bot operators can seek approval for specific automated editing tasks. Administrators and designated property creators can add new relationship types after community discussion reaches consensus. The Ontology WikiProject helps coordinate how the main branches of the graph fit together. [User access levels](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Wikidata:User_access_levels) · [Temporary accounts](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Help:Temporary_accounts) · [Bot policy](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Wikidata:Bots) · [Property creators](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Wikidata:Property_creators) · [Ontology WikiProject](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Wikidata:WikiProject_Ontology)

## OpenAlex

OpenAlex’s topics originated in clusters of works in the scholarly citation network. OpenAlex maintains a fixed set of topic IDs. It can rename topics or move them to another subfield and records these changes in a public log. Anyone can report a misnamed or misplaced topic by ID. [Topics](https://help.openalex.org/data/topics/) · [Hierarchy changes](https://help.openalex.org/data/topic-hierarchy-changes/)

OpenAlex’s classifier assigns up to three topics to a work from its title, abstract, and source. An author’s topics are computed from their attributed works. Authors can correct which works belong to a profile they have claimed. Site and organization curators can make these corrections on any author profile. [Topic assignments](https://help.openalex.org/data/topics/) · [Author topics](https://help.openalex.org/data/authors/) · [Curation permissions](https://help.openalex.org/data/curations/)
